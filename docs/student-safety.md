# Student Safety

TruePath AI is designed for students ages 10-18. The platform must protect emotional safety, identity development, privacy, and agency before optimizing for engagement or growth.

## Emotional safety principles

- Students should feel seen, not judged.
- Uncertainty should be treated as normal.
- Results should open possibilities, not close them.
- Challenge areas should be framed gently and constructively.
- The product should never make students feel behind, broken, or limited.

## Psychological considerations

Students are still forming identity, confidence, social belonging, and future imagination. Product experiences must avoid:

- Fixed identity labels
- Deficit-focused summaries
- Social comparison
- Fear-based motivation
- Manipulative streaks or pressure loops
- Over-personalized claims that sound clinical or diagnostic
- Language that could be internalized as permanent truth

## Age-sensitive experience rules

For younger students, ages 10-13:

- Use simple, concrete wording
- Keep choices low-pressure
- Avoid abstract personality claims
- Emphasize curiosity, play, and noticing

For older students, ages 14-18:

- Support autonomy and nuance
- Avoid implying college or career urgency
- Encourage reflection across school, relationships, interests, and wellbeing
- Present future possibilities as exploratory, not prescriptive

## Identity protection philosophy

TruePath AI should protect the student's right to change.

No profile, dashboard, AI response, or assessment result should define a student as a fixed kind of person. A student's history should be treated as context for reflection, not evidence for prediction.

## Safe product defaults

- Prefer private reflection by default.
- Share only summaries that are useful and respectful.
- Avoid dashboards that rank or compare students.
- Make sensitive information optional whenever possible.
- Allow students to revisit and revise reflections.
- Keep adult-facing language supportive, not evaluative.

## Emotional quality bar

Before shipping any student-facing feature, ask:

1. Could this make a student feel labeled?
2. Could this make a student feel behind or inferior?
3. Could this be misused by an adult to limit opportunity?
4. Does this preserve the student's agency and dignity?
5. Does this invite healthy exploration?

If the answer raises concern, the feature should be redesigned before implementation.
